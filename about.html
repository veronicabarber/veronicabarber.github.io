About Me

I am a first-generation student from Northeastern New York and a member of the Kanienʼkehá꞉ka. My intellectual life begins with two thinkers who seem, at first, unlikely companions: Thoreau’s Walden and Nietzsche’s amor fati. Together they point toward a mixture of hope and minimalism, and toward a labor of common respect and understanding. In political terms, this means a society able to answer pluralistic needs without flattening them.

I came to artificial intelligence through a practical question: what happens to a society when finance jobs are displaced? That question led me to study AI and to take seriously the repercussions of its continued advance for society and government. Alongside the theory, I am drawn to technology governance more broadly, including machine learning, alignment, and, most recently, the development of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems.

Academically, I am examining the use of AI in public decision-making and what it means for democratic legitimacy. At stake is whether we can rely on artificial intelligence to aid collective decisions without losing our humanism.
